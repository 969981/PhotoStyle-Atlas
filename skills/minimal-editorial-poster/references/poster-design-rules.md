# Poster Design Rules — Minimal Editorial

Source-specific design rules for `ivory-editorial-v1`. Every poster must satisfy the locked preset and these source-specific rules. The locked preset is in `references/style-presets.md`.

## One poster per source

- Exactly one poster per source photograph. Never combine multiple source photos.
- The poster is 3:4 (e.g. 1080 × 1440).
- The layout is a **strictly equal** top-photo / bottom-abstract split at exactly 50:50 — both regions have the exact same height. No tolerance.
- There is no divider, no title band, no label strip, no gap between regions. The two regions meet seamlessly edge-to-edge.
- The photographic region on top is the source photograph cropped to exactly 3:2 landscape (matching the top region's ratio: full canvas width × half canvas height). The photo fills the entire top region edge-to-edge — no borders, no margins, no letterboxing.
- The abstract region below fills the entire bottom region edge-to-edge, with its own composition sitting on the ivory background.
- All source aspect ratios (16:9, 4:3, 3:2, 1:1, portrait, etc.) are handled by cropping to 3:2 to fill the top region. The 50:50 split is non-negotiable.

## Photographic region — strict fidelity

### Strict rule
The photographic region must be exactly as in the source photograph, with **cropping as the only allowed modification**.

### Forbidden modifications
- No filters of any kind
- No color grading or color adjustment
- No stylization or painterly effects
- No retouching, object removal, or object addition
- No compositing or collage
- No rotation or perspective change
- No stretching or distortion
- No sharpening or blur beyond what is naturally in the photo

### What is preserved
- Subject identity
- Proportions and scale
- Perspective
- Materials and textures
- Lighting quality and direction
- Shadows
- Spatial relationships
- All text naturally present in the photo (signs, dates, slogans, labels, graffiti, plaques, markings — everything)

## Crop strategy — subject-focused

Both landscape and portrait sources are cropped to a 3:2 landscape window. Portrait sources always require this crop.

### Crop philosophy
The crop is **subject-focused**: it may reduce surrounding scene and background to strengthen focus on the primary subject. The goal is a clean, concentrated image where the subject reads clearly without distracting peripheral elements.

This is not a "tight crop" — it is a balanced crop that gives the subject breathing room but removes unnecessary context.

### Priority order for what to preserve
1. **Primary subject** — the main thing the photograph is about
2. **Identity information** — signs, text, distinctive features that identify the place or subject
3. **Key spatial relationships** — how the subject sits in its immediate environment

### Order for what may be discarded
1. **Distant background** — sky, far landscape, out-of-focus periphery
2. **Peripheral low-information areas** — blank walls, empty pavement, featureless ground
3. **Secondary subjects** — minor people, incidental objects, passing elements (only if they distract from the primary subject)

### Portrait-specific rule
Portrait sources are mandatory cropped to 3:2 landscape. Never use a left-right poster layout for portrait inputs. Select the strongest horizontal window that best preserves the primary subject and identity information.

### If no 3:2 window fits everything
Choose the window that preserves the primary subject and identity information most completely. Record any discarded secondary elements in the poster manifest. Never switch to a left-right layout.

## Abstract composition — minimal editorial

### Core principle
The abstract region reinterprets the subject's **most recognizable contours and structures** as a simplified abstract composition. The result is abstract — reduced to geometry — but **instantly recognizable** as the subject from the photograph above.

It is not a decorative pattern, not an illustration, not a collage. It is a **reductive distillation**: the essential visual DNA of the subject, arranged into a balanced, spacious composition.

### Visual language
- **Clean geometric forms** — rectangles, circles, arcs, lines, simple shapes that echo the subject's structure
- **Flat color fields** — solid, uniform color areas with no gradients, no shading, no 3D effects
- **Fine lines** — thin, precise lines for structural details, contours, or spatial markers
- **Generous negative space** — the ivory background is active and important; it gives the composition air
- **Balanced central composition** — elements are centered with generous margins, visually weighted but not crowded
- **Asymmetric balance** — perfect symmetry is not required; the composition should feel stable and considered
- **Photo-native text kept in place** — recognizable cultural text from the photo (dates, mottos, place names, slogans, plaques) may appear in the abstract region, always anchored to the structure it belongs to, at its original relative position

### Photo-native text within the abstract region
The abstract region may, and is encouraged to, preserve recognizable cultural text found in the photograph — dates (e.g. `1895`), mottos (e.g. `实事求是`), place names (e.g. `天津大学`), slogans, signage, and plaques — **as long as that text already exists in the photo**.

Rules for how that text appears:
- Text stays **anchored to the structure it belongs to** (a plaque on the wall, a date on the gate, a heading on the building), keeping its **original relative position** within that structure's abstraction.
- Text is **never lifted out and re-composed** as a separate typographic layout, centered title, or caption. It is part of the structure, not an independent graphic.
- Text remains **flat and minimal** like every other element — flat color, no shadows, no effects — so it reads as part of the abstraction, not as added design type.
- Text is legible enough to be recognized as the same text as in the photo, but it does not have to be typographically faithful to the original font.

This is **preservation in place**, not decoration: the word keeps its place in the structure, so the composition remains source-faithful rather than becoming a poster with new typography.

### Mood and reference points
- High-end editorial design (architecture magazines, luxury journals)
- Contemporary art exhibition posters
- Museum catalog covers
- Architectural graphic design
- Understated luxury
- Quiet, precise, unhurried

## Abstract element roles

Each abstract composition uses **4 to 7 elements**, selected from a role system. Three roles are mandatory; the rest are chosen from a candidate pool based on the source photograph's visual richness.

### Mandatory roles (always 3)

| Role | What it represents | How it appears |
|------|-------------------|---------------|
| **primary-form** | The most recognizable silhouette or mass of the subject — the dominant shape that makes it instantly identifiable | The largest abstract shape; typically a single geometric field or contour; the visual anchor of the composition |
| **secondary-structure** | The secondary structural elements — repeating modules, divisions, components that articulate the primary form | Smaller geometric forms, lines, or shapes that show how the primary form is organized; rhythmic, modular, architectural |
| **spatial-anchor** | The spatial logic — axis, horizon, baseline, grid, or a key spatial marker that grounds the composition | Often a single line (horizontal, vertical, or diagonal), a small geometric marker, or a subtle spatial reference that establishes scale and orientation |

### Optional pool (choose 1–4)

| Role | What it represents | When to choose it |
|------|-------------------|-------------------|
| **organic-accent** | A natural, soft-edged or organic form related to the subject — trees, plants, hills, clouds, water | The photo contains significant natural elements that interact with or frame the built/formal subject |
| **material-pattern** | A surface material expressed as pattern or texture — brick pattern, stone joints, wood grain, metal panels | The subject's material character is a strong visual feature worth distilling |
| **light-mass** | A large area of light or shadow expressed as a flat field — not realistic shading, but a graphic statement of light | The photograph has a strong light/shadow dynamic that is part of the subject's character |
| **detail-mark** | A fine detail, ornamental feature, or distinctive mark — carvings, railings, patterns, logos, plaques | There is one specific fine detail that carries meaning or identity and deserves a distilled presence |
| **native-text-mark** | Recognizable photo-native cultural text kept in place — a date, motto, place name, or slogan that belongs to a structure | The photo contains culturally significant text (e.g. `1895`, `实事求是`, `天津大学`) that should be preserved **at its original relative position**, not recomposed |
| **figure-silhouette** | A human figure distilled to silhouette or minimal form — as a scale reference or compositional element | People in the photo provide essential scale or have a strong compositional relationship to the subject |

### Selection principles
- **Fewer strong elements beat many weak ones.** If the source is simple, use 4 elements. If it is rich and complex, use up to 7.
- Every element must have a **clear origin** in the photograph. Nothing comes from outside the source.
- Elements are selected for **visual strength and recognizability**, not completeness. You are distilling, not cataloging.
- Each element should do a distinct job in the composition: anchor, structure, ground, accent, texture, light, detail, scale. Avoid redundant roles.

## Palette

### Background
- Fixed ivory: `#F3EBDD`
- This is the poster background and the background of the abstract region

### Color roles (3 mandatory + source-driven)

| Role | Description | Mandatory |
|------|-------------|-----------|
| **subject** | The dominant color of the primary subject form | Yes |
| **dark-anchor** | The darkest significant color — grounds the composition | Yes |
| **accent** | The most distinctive or vivid color — the "pop" color | Yes |
| **natural** | A green, earth, or foliage tone | Source-driven |
| **sky-water** | Blue or reflective tone from sky or water | Source-driven |
| **material** | A material-specific tone (brick, stone, wood, metal) | Source-driven |
| **highlight** | A very light tone for highlights or bright areas | Source-driven |
| **muted** | A subdued middle tone for subtle layering | Source-driven |

The source-driven pool above is illustrative, not exhaustive — any clearly distinct tone visible in the photograph may join the palette if it earns its place.

### Rules
- **All colors must be sampled from the source photograph.** Nothing is added from outside.
- The 3 mandatory roles (subject, dark-anchor, accent) are always present. Beyond them there is **no fixed count** — include only the distinct tones the photograph actually supports.
- **Restraint principle: fewer rather than more.** Every additional color must earn its place by doing real work in the composition. When in doubt, leave it out.
- The ivory background is separate and does not count as a palette color
- Colors should be clear, not muddy. Prefer clean, slightly desaturated tones that feel editorial.
- No gradients, no neon, no unrealistic saturation

## Composition principles for the abstract region

### Generous margins
The abstract elements sit within a frame of generous ivory margins. The composition does not touch the edges of the region. Think of it as a small artwork centered on a large page.

### Balanced central composition
Elements are roughly centered but not mechanically symmetric. The composition should feel stable, considered, and visually balanced. Asymmetry is fine as long as the overall weight feels right.

### Negative space is active
The ivory background is not "empty" — it is a design element. The spacing between shapes, the breathing room around the composition, and the ratio of form to space all matter. Aim for more space than form.

### Visual hierarchy
- `primary-form` is the largest and most dominant element
- `secondary-structure` elements are medium-sized and support the primary form
- `spatial-anchor` is usually the smallest or thinnest element
- Optional elements add richness but never compete with the primary form

### Flatness
Everything is flat. No shadows, no gradients, no 3D effects, no bevels, no textures applied to shapes (material patterns are expressed as flat geometric patterns, not realistic textures).

## Prompt construction order

Build the image instruction in this exact order:

1. **Crop specification** — subject-focused 3:2 landscape crop, what's preserved, what may be reduced
2. **Canvas and layout** — 3:4 poster, exactly 50:50 top-photo/bottom-abstract split (both regions strictly equal height), no divider, no gap, photo fills top region edge-to-edge at 3:2 ratio
3. **Photographic fidelity** — strict fidelity: no filters, no color changes, no retouching, only crop; all photo-native text preserved
4. **Abstract element list** — each element with its role and photo-origin description
5. **Color list** — each color with its role, all sampled from the photo; ivory background
6. **Style directive** — minimal editorial design, clean geometric forms, flat color fields, fine lines, generous negative space, balanced central composition, wide margins, museum-exhibition feel, understated luxury
7. **Prohibited** — no added text, no added decoration, no photo modification, no template feel, no 3D, no gradients, no neon, no complex illustration, no subject distortion, no cartoon, no commercial cliché

## What "instantly recognizable" means

The abstract composition should pass this test: if someone who has seen the original photograph looks at the abstract region alone, they should immediately understand what subject it depicts. It is not a literal depiction — it is a distilled, geometric, reductive version — but the subject's identity comes through clearly.

This is achieved by:
1. Faithfully capturing the primary-form silhouette and proportions
2. Including enough structural detail (secondary-structure) to convey the subject's organization
3. Using colors that are recognizably from the source
4. Preserving key spatial relationships through the spatial-anchor
5. Adding carefully chosen optional elements that carry specific character

## What to avoid

- **Template feel**: compositions that look like generic design templates rather than source-specific distillations
- **Over-decoration**: adding elements just to "fill space"
- **Complex illustration**: detailed representational drawing
- **3D rendering**: any volumetric, shaded, or perspectival rendering in the abstract region
- **Gradients and neon**: anything that feels commercial or trendy
- **Subject distortion**: changing the subject's proportions or identity to fit a design
- **Cartoon or stylized illustration**: anything that feels like a cartoon, icon, or mascot
- **Collage**: cutting and pasting photo fragments into the abstract region
- **Random geometric shapes**: shapes that don't trace back to something in the photo
