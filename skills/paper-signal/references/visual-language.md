# Paper Signal visual language

Paper Signal is a material system, not a beige template. Its identity comes from a quiet paper field, one image-bearing relation, sparse printed type, one chromatic signal, and a believable reproduction process. The subject decides the scale.

## The five-part kernel

Every image should resolve these five parts:

1. **Field** — paper with tonal life, fibres, absorption, and scan history.
2. **Image** — a landscape window, portrait, object, clipping, specimen, document fragment, or visual metaphor worth looking at.
3. **Type** — sparse language placed as printed matter, not a UI layer.
4. **Signal** — one chromatic intervention that changes the reading of the image.
5. **Process** — one physical reproduction logic that explains softness, grain, bleed, or misregistration.

If a result has paper and type but no meaningful image, it is unfinished. If it has an attractive image but no material causality, it is a filtered photograph rather than Paper Signal.

These are perceptual ranges. Do not measure faint paper noise as active content. Break a range when the user explicitly asks for another density or when preserving an edit target requires it.

## Composition quality

- Create one primary relation: image against color, two frames across a seam, object against measurement, face against a paper crop, or word against a fragment.
- Use asymmetric balance. A centered image may work, but perfect bilateral symmetry usually feels templated.
- Make at least one edge meaningful: a crop, torn fibre, ink boundary, plate offset, fold, or text collision.
- Keep empty space directional. It should lead toward, away from, or around the subject rather than surrounding it uniformly.
- Vary scale across a series. Shared material creates continuity; cloned geometry creates boredom.
- Let a landscape or portrait remain visually generous when it is the reason to look.

## Paper field

Treat paper as photographed material, not a hex color.

- Use warm ivory, gray-beige, cool copier white, pale blue-gray, or sun-aged cream.
- Show nonuniform fibres, faint mottling, edge-darkening, a soft fold, scan band, dust, or uneven absorption.
- Keep defects sparse and spatially varied. Uniform noise exposes a digital overlay.
- Use a flat orthographic scan or direct print view with diffuse light and no mockup shadow.
- Preserve tonal separation between paper and image. Muddy gray across the entire page destroys the signal.

## Reproduction process

Choose one lead process per image or series:

| Process | Valid character | Do not combine by default with |
|---|---|---|
| photocopy / xerox | toner bloom, clipped blacks, repeat ghost, soft edge | glossy photo finish, deep cinematic shadow |
| risograph | grain, dot gain, limited plate color, slight offset | perfect gradients, many saturated hues |
| newsprint halftone | visible dot structure, ink gain, soft detail | razor-sharp microdetail |
| letterpress | rough type edge, pressure variation, ink spread | photographic depth as the main effect |
| cyanotype / single-ink photo | deep blue plate, paper highlights, washed edges | multicolor commercial grading |
| faded film print | subdued grain, pale highlights, slight color drift | random heavy scratches and fake damage |

Paper aging can coexist with any one process. Do not stack every defect just because each sounds tactile.

## Image integration

- Print the image into the paper: allow paper highlights to show through, soften tonal extremes, and give edges a physical explanation.
- Preserve the subject's strongest recognisable structure before adding stylisation.
- Use partial colorization only when it clarifies the anchor. Keep the remainder in coherent grayscale, duotone, or faded color.
- Avoid a clean digital rectangle dropped onto vintage paper. Use one softened edge, plate overlap, fibre tear, print margin, or tonal bridge.
- Avoid stock-photo completeness. A decisive crop feels more authored than a fully described scene.

## Typography

Use type as a material voice.

- Pair a small old-book serif or restrained grotesk with typewriter/monospaced metadata.
- Use one short readable phrase, optionally one real date, place, number, or caption.
- Permit a drifting baseline, tight image-edge collision, faint ghost impression, imperfect registration, or partial crop.
- Keep names, values, and supplied wording exact.
- Prefer no text to atmospheric pseudo-text. Never invent Japanese/Korean characters, archive numbers, coordinates, weather, or signatures.
- Use large type only in `type-object`, where the word itself is the subject; keep its edges physically printed and its layout noncommercial.

## Chromatic signal

Use one main saturated hue. The signal may be:

- a partial photographic plate
- a paper window or irregular cutout
- one object or garment region
- a horizon, sky, shadow, or light field
- a short printed word
- a seam connecting two frames

Choose area by composition mode rather than forcing every image to 1% color. Keep the hue opaque enough to survive thumbnail viewing, but small enough that the paper and subject still lead.

Suggested inks:

| Ink | Hex | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Cobalt | `#1646B8` | nocturnal, archival, lucid |
| Ultramarine | `#173EA5` | memory, water, cool distance |
| Cyanotype blue | `#0B4F7A` | weather, coastline, architecture |
| Tomato red | `#D63224` | friction, warmth, interruption |
| Vermilion | `#D9472B` | human presence, dusk, print energy |
| Lemon yellow | `#E2C718` | found light, observation |
| Pear green | `#77A83B` | field note, botanical, object study |
| Magenta | `#C52872` | culture, intimacy, music |

Derive the hue from a meaningful subject feature when possible. Use cobalt only as the fallback.

## Series continuity

Lock across a series:

- paper family and aging level
- one lead reproduction process
- one type pairing
- one anchor hue or a tightly controlled hue progression
- edge and safe-zone behaviour
- overall texture intensity

Vary by card:

- composition mode
- image scale and crop
- subject route
- cluster position
- type behaviour
- color form

Use an accepted first image as a material reference, not as a layout stencil.

## Anti-AI craft checks

Reject or regenerate when the image shows:

- meaningless microtext, fake alphabets, invented dates, or faux archival authority
- arbitrary birds, moons, flowers, stairs, doors, and silhouettes unrelated to the brief
- evenly distributed texture, repeated scratches, random tape, coffee rings, or scrapbook clutter
- perfect vector rectangles, rounded UI cards, dashboard grids, or Swiss-poster spacing disguised with grain
- excessive empty space that does not create direction, tension, scale, or pause
- a subject reduced so far that it no longer rewards looking
- waxy faces, altered identity, impossible hands, warped architecture, or invented product details
- full-bleed stock photography, glossy campaign light, 3D depth, neon, or cinematic lens effects
- multiple saturated hues competing for attention

When a result feels generic, do not simply add more texture. Strengthen the subject crop, choose a sharper image–signal relation, remove decorative language, and make the physical process more coherent.
