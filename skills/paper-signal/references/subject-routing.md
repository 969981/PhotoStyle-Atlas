# Subject routing

Choose the subject route before choosing a preset or layout. The route protects what must remain recognisable; the composition mode decides how much of the page the image may occupy.

## Route table

| Subject route | Protect first | Preferred composition modes | Typical image share | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---:|---|
| `landscape` | horizon, weather, depth, one place-defining feature | `panorama-strip`, `photo-window`, `dual-frame` | 18%-45% | shrinking every scene into an unreadable thumbnail; generic mountain or bird symbols |
| `portrait` | identity, face geometry, expression, pose, hands when important | `portrait-archive`, `photo-window`, `dual-frame` | 22%-44% | text over eyes; face replacement; fashion-ad retouching; decorative anatomy |
| `object` | silhouette, material, scale cues, functional details | `specimen-plate`, `airy-fragment`, `dual-frame` | 12%-34% | glossy product-ad pedestal; invented logos; generic icon substitution |
| `architecture` | proportions, openings, vanishing lines, spatial rhythm | `photo-window`, `panorama-strip`, `dual-frame` | 22%-46% | impossible geometry; heavy collage hiding the space; real-estate-ad polish |
| `concept` | one metaphor, one emotional contradiction, one decisive visual relation | `airy-fragment`, `type-object`, `dual-frame` | 10%-30% | illustrating every sentence; literal icon soup; random surreal objects |
| `evidence` | exact values, labels, units, comparison meaning | `airy-fragment`, `dual-frame`, `specimen-plate` | 12%-30% | dashboard grids; decorative charts; numbers detached from meaning |
| `mixed-series` | each card's subject plus the shared story | vary by card; lock material system | varies | forcing every card into the cover layout |

Image share is the visibly active photographic, printed, or object-bearing area—not every faint mark on the paper. Use it as a range, not a pixel quota.

## Composition modes

### `airy-fragment`

Use for poems, compact concepts, quiet covers, and one small evidentiary relation.

- Keep about 72%-88% of the canvas as perceptually quiet paper.
- Build one 10%-24% cluster with a real image, clipping, specimen, or type-object relation.
- Let one phrase or metadata line sit outside the cluster only when it creates useful tension.

### `photo-window`

Use when the photograph itself carries atmosphere, identity, or place.

- Let one photo window occupy about 24%-45% of the canvas.
- Preserve a generous paper field around it instead of reducing the image until it becomes decorative.
- Crop decisively around one spatial or emotional fact. Keep the photo printed into the page, not floating as a glossy digital rectangle.

### `panorama-strip`

Use for horizons, streets, coastlines, weather, and architecture with directional movement.

- Use a horizontal or vertical strip occupying about 18%-36% of the canvas.
- Keep one uninterrupted line—horizon, road, roofline, shadow, or shoreline—legible.
- Allow one edge to dissolve through toner, halftone, or torn fibre; keep the other edge deliberate.

### `dual-frame`

Use for memory/present, near/far, before/after, detail/context, or two parts of one landscape.

- Use two related frames totalling about 22%-42% of the canvas.
- Change crop or tonal treatment between frames while preserving one shared axis.
- Avoid labelled corporate columns. Let a gap, overlap, seam, or line create the relation.

### `specimen-plate`

Use for objects, products, plants, food, tools, books, garments, and isolated architectural details.

- Place one object or a restrained pair on 12%-34% of the canvas.
- Preserve material cues and believable edges.
- Use a printed shadow, measurement tick, or tiny real caption only when it adds meaning.

### `portrait-archive`

Use for people, self-portraits, musicians, authors, and documentary figures.

- Keep the head and expression large enough to survive phone viewing: normally 22%-44% of the canvas.
- Preserve facial proportions, hairline, skin tone, expression, and pose unless the user requests a transformation.
- Put type beside the face or along the image edge; never across the eyes or mouth.
- Let the single color signal appear as a paper crop, garment region, shadow plate, or short title—not cosmetic face paint by default.

### `type-object`

Use only when words, a numeral, or a glyph truly are the subject.

- Keep type physically printed, imperfect, and spatially related to one small image or rule.
- One display word may lead, but avoid a polished campaign headline.
- Do not use this mode merely because a concrete image is difficult to imagine.

## Subject-specific transformations

### Landscape and weather

- Preserve atmospheric depth through tonal separation, not cinematic blur.
- Convert color selectively: a cyanotype sky, cobalt rain field, tomato-red sun, or pear-green vegetation may become the single ink signal.
- Prefer one lived detail—a window light, wet road, seawall, tree line, distant figure—over postcard completeness.
- Keep weather material: fog can become paper haze; rain can become scan streaks; glare can become unprinted paper.

### Portraits

- Treat identity preservation as blocking.
- Prefer crop, duotone, halftone, xerox softness, paper integration, and restrained plate offset over face regeneration.
- Keep skin texture human. Do not beautify into porcelain, sharpen eyes unnaturally, or invent jewellery, text, tattoos, or clothing marks.
- For a sensitive public figure, follow the runtime's image policy and prefer clearly editorial treatment.

### Objects and products

- Let wear, glaze, wood grain, fabric weave, metal patina, or paper edge remain visible.
- Separate editorial object study from advertising: no logo lockup, price badge, glossy pedestal, CTA, or impossible perfection unless explicitly requested.
- Use a detail frame only when it reveals a second material fact.

### Architecture and interiors

- Preserve verticals, openings, proportions, and the logic of light.
- Use cropped elevations, window rhythms, stair fragments, or floor shadows as the anchor.
- Do not replace real spatial character with generic brutalism, Scandinavian minimalism, or Japanese interiors unless present in the source.

### Concepts and articles

- Write a one-sentence visual thesis before prompting.
- Choose one noun, one action, and one tension. Example: `door / stays open / night presses inward`.
- If the metaphor needs more than two main objects to explain, simplify it.

### Evidence and data

- Keep the exact decision signal and remove explanatory furniture.
- Translate a comparison into paper relations: two strips, one selected specimen, a repeated photocopy, a stamped sequence, or one highlighted row.
- Keep the full explanation in the caption or report. A zine page is not a dashboard wearing grain.

## Existing-photo decision

Choose the least invasive transformation that achieves the brief:

1. `reframe` — preserve pixels; change crop, paper field, and surrounding type.
2. `print-transfer` — preserve subject and crop; convert tonal/color process into xerox, riso, cyanotype, halftone, or letterpress-like print.
3. `editorial-composite` — preserve subject identity; combine one context crop or paper fragment.
4. `scene-transform` — change weather, time, or setting only when the user explicitly requests it.

Do not jump to scene transformation when a restrained print transfer would create the desired quality.
